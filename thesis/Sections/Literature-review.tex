\section{Literature review}
\label{sec:litreview}

\subsection{Single-cell foundation models}

Transformer models pretrained on large single-cell corpora --- scGPT~\cite{scgpt},
Geneformer~\cite{geneformer} --- treat a cell's expression profile as a sequence and transfer to
downstream tasks by fine-tuning. The ambition is often stated as building a ``virtual
cell''~\cite{bunne}: a model whose \emph{in silico} perturbation responses are trustworthy enough to
substitute for screening.

\subsection{Cell2Sentence: the backbone}
\label{sec:lit-c2s}

Cell2Sentence~\cite{c2s} takes the most literal route to adapting a language model to
transcriptomics: it serialises a cell as a \emph{cell sentence}, the gene symbols written in
descending order of expression, and fine-tunes an ordinary pretrained language model on the
resulting text. C2S-Scale~\cite{c2sscale} extends this to larger backbones and to
perturbation-prediction tasks, and supplies the \texttt{C2S-Scale-Pythia-1b} checkpoint used
throughout this thesis.

The representation discards expression magnitude and keeps only rank, and C2S justifies this with a
reconstruction argument: expression is recoverable from rank through a fitted log-linear model
$e_i = a_d\log(r_i) + b_d$, achieving $R^2 = 0.815$ against original expression on PBMC data. Rank,
they conclude, ``encodes expression in a consistent, reversible way across datasets''.

That claim is correct, and this thesis does not dispute it. What it shows is that the claim is
\emph{beside the point} for drug discrimination, in a way that connects directly to the dispute
reviewed below. Reconstruction fidelity is an \emph{absolute-accuracy} criterion --- how close is
the recovered profile to the true one --- and Section~\ref{sec:res-metrics} shows that absolute
accuracy on this task is dominated by the component every condition shares. A representation can
therefore preserve expression almost perfectly and still discard nearly all of the drug-specific
signal, because the preserved information is the part that does not distinguish drugs. Measured
directly (Section~\ref{sec:res-encoding}), $83\%$ of the ranked tokens are identical between any two
drugs in the same context. The fix that follows is not to abandon cell sentences but to change what
the rank encodes.

\subsection{Perturbation response prediction}

Dedicated architectures include scGen~\cite{scgen}, which applies a latent-space arithmetic shift;
CPA~\cite{cpa}, which disentangles perturbation and covariate factors; and GEARS~\cite{gears}, which
uses a gene--gene knowledge graph to extrapolate to unseen genetic perturbations. These are largely
developed on genetic perturbation atlases (Perturb-seq~\cite{replogle},~\cite{norman}), with
scPerturb~\cite{scperturb} providing harmonised benchmarks.

\subsection{The dispute: do these models beat uninformative baselines?}
\label{sec:lit-dispute}

The field currently contains an unresolved and unusually sharp disagreement, and this thesis is
best understood as an attempt to adjudicate a corner of it.

\paragraph{The case against.} A series of benchmarks reports that deep perturbation models do not
beat trivial predictors. Ahlmann-Eltze, Huber and Anders~\cite{ahlmanneltze} report that
deep-learning-based gene perturbation effect prediction does not yet outperform simple linear
baselines. Csendes et al.~\cite{csendes} reach a similar conclusion for foundation cell models on
post-perturbation RNA-seq. Bendidi et al.~\cite{bendidi} put it most bluntly in a title: one PCA
still rules them all. Kernfeld et al.~\cite{kernfeld} concur in a broad comparison of
expression-forecasting methods. The pattern is not confined to transcriptomic readouts: on scalar
drug sensitivity, Hauptmann and Kramer~\cite{hauptmann} hold the experimental protocol fixed across
architectures and find that the measured differences between them largely dissolve. And
DrEval~\cite{dreval}, reviewed in detail below, finds that deep models barely outperform a naive
predictor of mean drug and cell-line effects. The recurring result across all of them is that a
\emph{per-perturbation mean} is remarkably hard to beat.

\paragraph{The case for.} Miller et al.~\cite{miller} argue that these conclusions ``largely stem
from limitations of benchmarking metrics, not from the models themselves''. Their argument is
methodological rather than rhetorical. They formalise the use of positive and negative controls in
perturbation benchmarking, introduce a new positive control --- the \emph{interpolated duplicate},
which interpolates between a technical duplicate and the mean baseline with weights given by
per-gene differential-expression significance --- and define a quantitative measure of metric
calibration, the \emph{Dynamic Range Fraction} (\DRF). Applying this across 14 Perturb-seq datasets
and 13 metrics, they find that widely used metrics such as MSE and control-referenced Pearson
correlation are often poorly calibrated, whereas weighted and rank-based alternatives --- weighted
MSE and weighted $R^2\Delta$~\cite{mejia}, and Normalised Inverse Rank~\cite{perturbench} --- are
consistently calibrated. Under those calibrated metrics, they report, deep learning models
\emph{do} outperform mean, control and linear baselines, and in some combination-prediction tasks
even the additive baseline.

\paragraph{What the disagreement is actually about.} Both sides agree that the standard metrics are
defective. They disagree about what remains once the defect is corrected: whether calibrated
evaluation reveals a real signal that miscalibration was hiding, or merely relocates the same null.
This makes the dispute empirically decidable rather than interpretive --- but only within the
setting where it is tested.

\paragraph{The gap this thesis addresses.} Both Miller et al.\ and Ahlmann-Eltze et al.\ study
\textbf{genetic} perturbation (Perturb-seq: gene inhibition or activation). DrEval studies
\textbf{drug sensitivity} --- a scalar potency such as IC$_{50}$ or AUC predicted from cell-line
omics. None of them studies what this thesis studies: \textbf{drug} perturbation, predicted as a
full transcriptomic response, at single-cell resolution, by an autoregressive generative language
model. The tasks differ in ways that plausibly matter --- a genetic knockout is a designed,
near-binary intervention with a named target gene, whereas a small molecule acts at a dose, hits
multiple proteins, and (as Section~\ref{sec:res-blind} reports) is statistically inert in $27\%$ of
conditions.

\paragraph{Our position, and why it takes this form.} We adopt the \emph{proponents'} instrument.
Section~\ref{sec:res-metrics} ports Miller's \DRF{} framework wholesale and evaluates it on drug
perturbation, and their central methodological claim is confirmed emphatically: metric choice
dominates, and in our setting only one of five metrics is calibrated at all. We then evaluate the
model on that metric --- \NIR, the rank-based metric their analysis endorses --- and find it at
chance.

Siding with the sceptics using the proponents' own instrument is the strongest available form of the
argument, because it cannot be answered by the proponents' own objection. The conclusion is not that
Miller et al.\ are wrong; on genetic perturbation their evidence stands, and their framework is what
made our result quotable. It is that their conclusion does not extend to this task, and
Section~\ref{sec:res-lookup} offers a structural reason why it might not.

\subsection{DrEval and the six pathologies}
\label{sec:lit-dreval}

DrEval~\cite{dreval} audits drug response prediction and identifies six obstacles to clinical
translation: non-reproducible models, data leakage causing weak generalisation, pseudoreplication,
biased evaluation metrics, missing ablation studies, and inconsistent response data. Three of them
structure the methodology of Section~\ref{sec:methods}, and we adopt its vocabulary throughout.

\paragraph{Pseudoreplication.} Following Hurlbert~\cite{hurlbert}, treating multiple measurements of
one unit as independent. DrEval notes that GDSC2's $395{,}025$ measurements derive from only $969$
cell lines and $287$ drugs. Our structure is identical: hundreds of thousands of cells resolving to
a few thousand conditions over $50$ cell lines. All uncertainty here is therefore a clustered
bootstrap over cell lines.

\paragraph{Biased evaluation.} DrEval frames this as a version of Simpson's paradox: because drugs
act at vastly different concentrations, most variance is \emph{between}-drug, so a model that
memorises each drug's mean explains most of it. Their illustration is a global Pearson correlation
of $0.90$ against an average per-drug Pearson of $0.01$. Section~\ref{sec:res-metrics} reports the
transcriptomic analogue, and Section~\ref{sec:methods-metrics} explains why a discrimination metric
evaluated \emph{within} a comparison set is immune by construction.

\paragraph{Application-aware splits.} DrEval names four: Leave-Pairs-Out (LPO, missing-value
imputation), Leave-Cell-Lines-Out (LCO, personalised medicine), Leave-Drugs-Out (LDO, drug design),
and Leave-Tissue-Out (LTO, drug repurposing). Our holdout maps directly --- \texttt{unseen\_combo}
is LPO, \texttt{unseen\_drug} is LDO --- and we use their names in
Section~\ref{sec:methods-holdout}. The mapping carries a warning we take seriously: DrEval reports,
citing~\cite{partin}, that good LPO and LCO performance is reachable by predicting each drug's
average response across the training cell lines. Our strongest positive result
(Section~\ref{sec:res-generalisation}) is an LPO result. Section~\ref{sec:res-lookup} measures that
baseline rather than assuming it away.

\paragraph{Missing ablations.} DrEval's remedy is randomisation and robustness testing. The scramble
ablation of Section~\ref{sec:methods-controls} is exactly such a test, specialised to the drug
token, and it is the instrument on which every drug-use claim in this thesis rests.

\subsection{Connectivity mapping and signature transfer}

The Connectivity Map programme~\cite{cmap,l1000} built a drug-discovery methodology on a specific
premise: that a compound induces a characteristic transcriptional signature stable enough across
cellular contexts that querying a reference database by signature similarity is informative. The
L1000 platform~\cite{l1000} operationalised this at scale, and its 978-gene landmark set defines the
gene panel used here (intersected with Tahoe's measured genes, giving $946$).

That premise --- \emph{a drug's signature transfers across cell lines} --- is normally assumed
rather than measured. Section~\ref{sec:res-transfer} measures it directly, noise-corrected, at
single-cell resolution. The result determines whether a conditional model has anything left to
predict once the per-drug constant is accounted for, and therefore bears directly on the dispute in
Section~\ref{sec:lit-dispute}: if the per-drug constant is nearly the whole signal, then a
per-perturbation mean being hard to beat is not a benchmarking artifact at all.

\subsection{Interpretability: decodable versus used}
\label{sec:lit-interp}

Linear probing establishes that information is \emph{present}, not that it is \emph{used}. The
distinction is central here, since we find both: the drug is decodable at $82\%$ and simultaneously
ignored by generation. Elazar et al.~\cite{gurnee} make the methodological case for treating these
as separate questions: probe accuracy licenses no conclusion about behaviour, and the way to ask
whether a property is used is to remove it from the representation and measure what the model then
does. Their amnesic-probing design is the one Section~\ref{sec:res-mechanism} adopts, with one
addition --- a matched-norm control, since an intervention that fails to move the output proves
nothing unless a matched random intervention does.

What such an intervention can find is shaped by how a network spends its representational capacity.
Elhage et al.~\cite{superposition} show that models reserve dedicated directions for features the
computation needs on every forward pass and push the remainder into superposition, where a feature
stays linearly recoverable without occupying an axis of its own. We take from this a prediction
rather than a finding. If drug identity is needed only for occasional, context-dependent
composition, it may be represented richly and still sit outside whatever subspace the generation
readout actually consults --- while cell-line identity, which any plausible emitted profile
requires, should not. That asymmetry is testable, and testing it is the subject of
Section~\ref{sec:res-mechanism}.

\subsection{Optimal transport for state matching}

Entropic regularisation with Sinkhorn iterations~\cite{cuturi} makes optimal transport tractable at
scale, and Waddington-OT~\cite{schiebinger} established the approach for single-cell trajectories.
We use it in Section~\ref{sec:res-epsilon} not as a proposed method but as a \emph{controlled
sweep}, since the regularisation parameter interpolates between two target constructions already
tested independently. Couplings are computed in the released Tahoe scVI latent~\cite{scvi}.
